Prioritization Is About Saying No: And Saying It Early

Saying no used to feel like failure. It took me years to learn that it is the job, and that doing it early, with clarity and empathy, is what keeps a roadmap strategic. This essay first ran on Substack in July 2025. This edition lives next to the record it came from.

By Douglas Cirillo · First published on Substack, July 8, 2025 · Site edition, November 2025

Why did saying no feel like failure?

Early in my career, saying no felt like failure.

I wanted to be the person who said yes. The one who moved fast, earned trust, and got things done. I thought that’s what made me effective.

But I’ve learned that speed without focus creates waste. And that the job of product leadership isn’t just about keeping things moving. It’s about making sure what moves actually matters.

That starts with knowing when to say no, and how to say it well.

Why is the hard call so easy to defer?

No one wants to be the blocker.

Especially not when teams are moving fast, expectations are high, and resources are thin. It’s easier to defer the hard call. To buy time. To say yes now and figure it out later.

I’ve done that. I’ve over-explained. Avoided the confrontation. Even said yes to things I knew didn’t fit, just to avoid slowing momentum or damaging trust.

But that’s a short-term play. And the cost always catches up.

The truth is: saying no is part of the job. And doing it early is what separates momentum from noise.

What makes a good no?

At Sotheby’s, we were in the middle of a massive transformation: rebuilding the platform, launching a CDP, realigning content strategy, standing up analytics, and overhauling internal workflows. Everyone had priorities. Everyone had asks.

Some were good. Some were urgent. Very few were aligned.

I learned quickly that a good “no” isn’t about shutting the door. It’s about opening the right conversation.

A strong “no” is:

  • Grounded in context. Tied to a clear roadmap, outcome, or constraint.
  • Framed with empathy. Acknowledging the ask and what’s behind it.
  • Paired with transparency. Explaining the why, not just delivering the verdict.

Done well, it doesn’t feel like rejection. It feels like direction.

What happens before the pushback?

At Lindblad, I had to push back on plenty of feature requests. Some came from sales. Some from marketing. Others from the C-suite. Most weren’t inherently bad ideas. They just didn’t fit the moment.

Instead of defaulting to no, I tried to understand the core need. What were they solving for? Was there a version of the request we could test or reframe?

Sometimes we A/B tested smaller variants. Sometimes we parked ideas for later. But either way, the teams felt heard. And when people feel heard, they’re more likely to stay engaged, even if the answer isn’t what they hoped.

That kind of dialogue protects trust. And trust protects velocity.

What is prioritization actually protecting?

I don’t naturally love saying no. I like momentum. I like being the person who clears the path.

But product leadership isn’t just about building. It’s about protecting what’s worth building.

If you say yes to everything, the roadmap becomes a wishlist. Strategy turns into hope. And your team gets burned out chasing priorities that never stack up.

That doesn’t mean being rigid.

It means being consistent. Transparent. Willing to ask the hard questions early, before scope creeps or clarity dissolves.

Why is prioritization a conversation?

The best prioritization frameworks aren’t just tools. They’re practices.

They create shared language. They give stakeholders a way to understand how decisions are made, even when they don’t agree. They reduce noise and elevate signal.

But only if you use them as a way to connect, not deflect.

At its best, prioritization isn’t about saying no to people.

It’s about saying yes to the right things, for the right reasons, at the right time.

And doing it early enough that everyone stays aligned.
