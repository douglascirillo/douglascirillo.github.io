Build It With Them, Not For Them

A strategy can be tight, the roadmap locked, the business case solid, and still nothing moves. Douglas Cirillo learned why at Madison Square Garden, then built the opposite way at Sotheby’s: with people, not for them. This essay first ran on Substack in July 2025. This edition lives next to the record it came from.

By Douglas Cirillo · First published on Substack, July 29, 2025 · Site edition, November 2025

Why does a locked strategy still stall?

There’s a moment in every transformation project when the strategy is tight, the roadmap is locked, and the business case looks solid.

And yet, nothing moves.

Not because the vision is flawed. Not because the tech is wrong. But because the people who have to make it real don’t feel connected to it.

They don’t believe in it yet.

Or worse, they don’t see themselves in it at all.

What did I get wrong about leadership?

Early in my career, I thought leadership meant showing up with the solution. That my job was to bring clarity, drive direction, and lead from the front.

But clarity without connection doesn’t get you far.

I’ve been the new guy, hired to turn the ship. I’ve walked into rooms where no one knew me, or worse, where they knew just enough to assume the worst. And in those moments, confidence only gets you so far.

If people don’t trust you, they won’t follow you. Even if you’re right.

What I’ve learned, through hard lessons and false starts, is this:

You don’t earn buy-in by being right.

You earn it by building belief.

Where did I get it wrong first?

At Madison Square Garden, I led the shift from a monolithic CMS to a composable stack. On paper, it was the right move: technically, operationally, strategically.

But we didn’t bring people in early. We assumed alignment. We moved fast and communicated sparingly.

When resistance came, we had no foundation of trust to stand on. And that lack of belief stalled everything.

I still believe in the strategy. But I got the approach wrong.

What changed at Sotheby’s?

When I joined Sotheby’s, I started small. I looked for signals of care, for pockets of momentum.

The digital marketing team had scrappily built a CRM system together to manage broken workflows. It wasn’t pretty, but it showed initiative. They were trying to solve the problem already.

So we built together:

  • Integrated a CDP to unify customer data.
  • Cleaned up the stack and made campaign execution more efficient.
  • Shared better insights across editorial, product, and sales.
  • Delivered wins that leadership actually noticed.

And slowly, the tide turned.

Teams started asking to join. Product got interested. Editorial raised their hand. Because they didn’t feel something was being forced on them.

They felt invited in.

Why does belief compound?

That’s the shift.

Not just building for others, but with them.

Belief compounds when people feel seen. When they’re part of the process. When they can point to something and say: we did that.

You don’t need everyone on day one. You need early champions. The ones willing to test, to build, to co-own the outcome.

If you invest in those relationships, if you make their wins visible, others follow.

Not because they were told to.

Because they want to.

How do I lead now?

This isn’t a soft skill.

It’s not a stakeholder management tactic.

It’s the only way real transformation sticks.

If you want to change a system, you need to start with the people inside it. You need to listen before you pitch. You need to earn belief, not demand it.

That’s what I try to do now, every time.

It is the core of my digital experience strategy practice.

Because I’ve been on both sides of the equation. I’ve felt like an afterthought. And I’ve also felt what it’s like to be part of something that actually includes you.

The difference is everything.
