Data First, AI Second, Now With Agents As The Proof

I have held this line since 2020: data first, AI second. At Sotheby’s and Lindblad, the sequence never changed. Definition and signals first. Tools followed. Results after that. Agents did not change the rule. They raised the stakes, and gave the data a new audience that extracts, compares, and decides. This essay first ran on Substack in November 2026.

What did I get wrong about agents?

Early in my career, I thought a new tool could fix a messy system. I thought that more than once. With agents, I thought it again.

I thought agents were the solve for everything. Put one on top of the stack, it would just know.

It doesn’t. It can’t.

Agents are garbage in, garbage out. They just scale it faster, in front of customers.

That is the part I got wrong. Agents only scale good decisions when the data underneath is well managed and structured. If it is not, an agent will not save you. It will expose you. Then it will recommend someone else.

I learned that the slower way.

What did Sotheby’s and Lindblad teach me?

At Sotheby’s, the customer data platform only worked when we did the unglamorous work first. We agreed on what a customer actually was. Shared KPIs. Shared language. Governance and reporting. Before we argued about tooling. The platform was not the strategy. The strategy built the need for the platform. That work contributed to digital revenue growing from $800M to $8B+.

At Lindblad, same sequence. We did not try to capture everything. We focused on signals that mattered to bookings, content engagement, lead quality, and journey progression. One useful signal at a time. Phased clarity, not a grand view.

Definition and signals first. Tools followed.

That alignment showed up as bookings up 30%.

You can’t activate what you don’t trust. A customer data platform is a product, not a project. Fuzzy definition, fragmented identity, stale catalog, activation just spreads the fuzziness faster.

Agents don’t change that rule. They raise the stakes.

What does an agent need to decide well?

The audience is no longer only human. An agent is now an audience for your product data and your experience. It doesn’t browse like a person. It doesn’t feel your brand. It extracts, compares, and decides.

For an agent to decide well, it needs a governed, machine-readable version of your product and your offer, with stable identity behind it. Which product, which variant, what price, what availability, what policy, from a source you govern.

Without that, an agent falls back to what it can find. Public pages. Third-party catalogs. Training data. Someone else’s cleaner version of a similar product. Not a technology failure. A data failure, with a new audience watching.

Building a digital experience and growth organization from scratch taught me the same sequence. Customer definition and outcomes first. Shared language and metrics. Prove value in one place, with one team, before you ask the whole organization to believe. Early wins recruit the next team. That method worked before agents. Agents now test it, every day, in public.

What is the readiness sequence?

Here is the readiness sequence, in plain terms. No vendor names. The sequence matters more than the stack. The practice page carries the working version.

  • First, govern the product and customer graph. One definition of the customer. One definition of the product. Stable identifiers that don’t change when a campaign changes.
  • Second, resolve identity. One customer, not fragments across web, email, mobile, and service.
  • Third, separate stable product facts from volatile offer state. The product is what it is. Price, inventory, and promotions change. Tangle those together, and an agent can surface last week’s price or a discontinued variant.
  • Fourth, expose structured catalog and content. Not a prettier page. A machine-readable model an agent can cite, compare, and act on, with the same facts a human would see.
  • Fifth, set intentional agent access. Consent signals. Approval caps. An audit trail. A decision about which agents can see what, made on purpose, not by default.
  • Sixth, measure agent journeys separately. If you only measure human traffic, you will miss when an agent skips you, recommends a competitor, or completes a purchase you never saw coming.

That is the whole test. You can run it on Monday.

What is the Monday test?

Ask an agent, as a customer would, for a product you carry. Does it return the right product, the right variant, the current price and availability, from a governed source? Or a close match, a stale price, or someone else’s product?

If it can’t see a governed, machine-readable version of your product and offer, it will recommend someone else’s. Not a prompt problem. A readiness problem.

Data first, AI second. I have held that line since 2020, before agents were the conversation. Agents didn’t change it. They proved it.

Agents don’t fix messy data. They expose it, and they scale it.

The Monday test is the shortest version of the whole argument.
