The Hardest Part (of Strategy) Is Letting Go

This lesson cost me. Early in my career at Forbes, I confused being right with being effective, and it ended my time there. What followed, at Sotheby’s, Lindblad, and Gangverk, is the record on this site: strategy as a shared path, not a solo argument. This essay first ran on Substack in September 2025.

By Douglas Cirillo · First published on Substack, September 23, 2025 · Site edition, November 2025

What is the hardest part of strategy?

For a long time, I thought the hardest part of strategy was getting it right.

Now I know it is letting go.

Letting go of ideas you believed in.

Letting go of being the one with the answers.

Letting go of control, even when you are the one driving the work forward.

It took me longer than I care to admit to learn that. And I am still learning.

When does conviction get in the way?

Earlier in my career, I struggled with disagreement. Not because I did not expect it, but because I did not always know how to sit with it. I had conviction. I had strong instincts. And I believed that if I saw the path clearly, it was my job to fight for it.

At Forbes, that instinct got the better of me.

There was a direction I disagreed with. Instead of committing to the plan, I resisted. I thought I was protecting the work. In reality, I was making it harder for the team to move forward. That friction slowed us down and fractured trust. Eventually, it led to my departure.

I was not wrong to care. I was not wrong to believe in my perspective. But I was wrong to think that being right was the same as being effective.

How do you learn to disagree and commit?

Since then, I have made it a personal goal to get better at “disagree and commit.”

It is not easy for me. I still wrestle with it. But I have seen what happens when you create space for other ideas, when you commit to the shared path, even if it was not yours. I have also seen the damage when you do not.

At Sotheby’s, that lesson helped me partner more effectively. I did not try to bulldoze my way to alignment. I picked the right allies. I showed progress. I let the work speak for itself, and people came along when they were ready.

At Lindblad, I learned to frame strategy through the lens of each team’s goals. Marketing cared about ROI. Sales cared about lead efficiency. Product cared about feasibility. The roadmap was not mine. It belonged to all of us.

What does stepping back look like?

Even more recently, working with clients at Gangverk, I try to remind myself: not every battle is mine to win. Sometimes the biggest impact I can make is by stepping back. Listening more. Framing the tradeoffs. Trusting the team to move forward, even if I would go a different route.

That is what real strategic maturity looks like.

It is not about avoiding conflict. It is about not letting conflict define your contribution.

What is the practice of letting go?

The best strategy often is not Plan A.

It is the one your team can align behind, execute on, and learn from together.

So yes, the hardest part of strategy is not knowing what to do.

It is knowing when to let go.

I am still learning. But I am getting better at it.

Thanks for reading,
Doug
