Composable Is a Culture, Not Just a Stack

Composable sounds like an architecture decision. It is a culture decision. Douglas Cirillo on the rollout that failed at MSG, the different tack that worked at Sotheby’s, and why adoption is earned, never installed. This essay first ran on Substack in August 2025. This edition lives next to the record it came from.

By Douglas Cirillo · First published on Substack, August 5, 2025 · Site edition, November 2025

Why is composable cultural, not just technical?

“Composable architecture” sounds smart. Sounds progressive. Looks great on a slide.

But too often, the conversation stops at the tech.

We talk MACH. We diagram microservices. We show off flexibility and speed.

And then we try to implement it like it’s just another upgrade.

That’s where it falls apart.

Composable isn’t just about infrastructure. It’s a mindset shift.

It demands more alignment, not less. More collaboration. More empathy.

You can’t brute-force your way into adoption. You have to earn it.

I learned this the hard way at MSG.

We were replacing a monolithic Adobe stack with a modular, MACH-aligned architecture. It was the right move: cloud-native, API-first, headless. All the buzzwords, all the checkboxes.

But the rollout failed.

Not because the strategy was wrong. Because the approach was.

We treated it like an IT initiative. Told people what was changing. Didn’t bring them in. Didn’t start with shared goals or early adopters.

And without belief, there was no momentum.

Does modularity apply to teams too?

The beauty of composable is modularity.

But that logic doesn’t stop at your stack. It applies to how you build your teams, how you structure your efforts, and how you grow capabilities over time.

Done right, a composable approach lets you start small and scale smart, swapping pieces in and out as the business evolves. You’re not locked into a one-size-fits-all model. You can build incrementally, one domain or team at a time, based on actual needs and readiness.

That’s how transformation sticks:

By aligning tech flexibility with team flexibility.

Each unit can move at its own pace, plug in, play forward, and iterate as goals shift and the organization grows.

What did Sotheby’s do differently?

At Sotheby’s, I took a different tack.

I started with the team that had hacked together their own CRM just to get by, marketing. They were eager. Resourceful. Scrappy.

We didn’t start with the full stack. We started with their pain.

We integrated a CDP to clean the data.

Tied in the campaign stack.

Added analytics where it would immediately show impact.

We built alongside them. Not on top of them.

And when it started working, word spread.

Editorial saw engagement rise. Execs saw ROI clarity. Teams that were once skeptical started leaning in, not because they were told to, but because the early wins spoke their language.

How is adoption earned?

Composable done right isn’t a stack rollout. It’s an invitation.

To experiment. To collaborate. To think differently.

But that invitation has to come with proof. With empathy. With patience.

If you lead with architecture, you’ll lose people.

If you lead with outcomes, you’ll earn believers.

It’s not slower. It’s smarter.

And the payoff is a stack, and a team, that can actually scale.

What is the real value of composable?

You can’t drop in a headless CMS and expect magic.

You have to rewire how people think about building.

That’s not easy. It’s not fast. But it’s worth it.

Because the real value of composable isn’t flexibility.

It’s alignment.

And the only way to get there is together.
