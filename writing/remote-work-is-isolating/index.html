Remote Work Is Isolating. And I’m Feeling It.

A personal one, from July 2025. I was working from New Jersey with a team in Iceland, and the math of remote work had stopped working for me. The flexibility was real. So was the isolation. This essay first ran on Substack in July 2025.

By Douglas Cirillo · First published on Substack, July 22, 2025 · Site edition, November 2025

What does flexibility cost?

Remote work has its perks. No commute. Fewer distractions. A wider hiring net. And let’s be honest, a little more freedom from the politics and performance theater of the office.

But flexibility isn’t free.

It comes with tradeoffs, ones we don’t always admit until they start creeping in. The erosion of structure. The slow bleed of energy. The constant tether between work and home. And the isolation.

Even the label says it: remote.

Where did the line between work and life go?

When I was younger, I didn’t think much about separation. I liked moving fast. I didn’t need guardrails, I needed green lights.

But now, with a partner, with kids, with more complexity in both work and life, I’ve come to value boundaries. I’ve come to need them.

Not just to block off hours in a calendar, but to protect different versions of myself.

Because without that separation, the roles blur.

There’s no commute to decompress. No moment of reset between “on” at work and present at home. Just one tab closing and another set of responsibilities opening, with no breathing room in between.

Why isn’t it just a time issue?

And that lack of transition isn’t just a time issue. It’s a mental load issue.

I’ve noticed I’m less patient after work. More reactive. More drained.

It’s not because I don’t love the chaos of family life, I do. But shifting gears instantly, without a buffer, has a cost. And I feel it more with each passing month.

The Sunday scaries don’t just come on Sundays anymore. They’re a baseline.

And I know I’m not alone in that.

What doesn’t translate virtually?

Some things don’t translate virtually.

You can’t catch tone in a Teams chat. You can’t read the room on Zoom. You can’t riff on an idea over lunch or get pulled into a 10-minute hallway conversation that turns into a game-changing idea.

I miss those moments. And more importantly, I learned in those moments.

At LexisNexis, MSG, Sotheby’s, yes, the projects were ambitious. But what made them special was the team dynamic. The trench mentality. The late nights, bad pizza, good jokes, and shared struggle that turned coworkers into collaborators, and collaborators into friends.

That’s where growth happened. Not just in strategy decks, but in shared momentum.

What do I miss, exactly?

I’m not the loudest in the room. I won’t work the room at a party. But I love working with sharp, driven humans. I crave that push-pull of real-time collaboration.

Right now, I don’t have that.

My team is great. Talented. Kind. Thoughtful.

But they’re in Iceland. Together. In rooms. On whiteboards. Inside a rhythm.

And I’m in New Jersey. Outside of it.

I contribute. I deliver. I’m thanked.

But I’m not in it. And I feel that.

What have I learned, and what do I want?

I’ve learned I’m not built for permanent isolation.

I’m not a pure consultant. I’m a builder. A listener. A synthesizer. Someone who thrives when there’s mutual energy and shared presence.

That doesn’t mean I need five days a week in an office.

But hybrid? In-person rhythm? Having people around to think with, move with, build with? Yes, please.

The next chapter, whatever and wherever it is, needs that.

It needs people.

Because that’s where the real work happens. Not just on screens. But in rooms. In energy. In context. In connection.
