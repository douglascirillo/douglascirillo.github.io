How this site runs

Douglas Cirillo is Head of Digital Experience and Growth at Vanguard. This page is the colophon for his personal site: the stack it runs on, how it is measured, and the team and rules behind it.

This site is a working demo. It is built and run the way I say digital teams should work: a small stack, data first, and a human making the decisions.

What is it built on?

Hand-written HTML. No framework, no CMS, no build step. Every page’s content is in the HTML itself, readable with JavaScript off; JavaScript layers on top for the interactive parts. Lists whose entries lead to pages of their own render as preview cards by default; reference lists inside a page stay text. The site is hosted on GitHub Pages and every change is a git commit, so the full history of what changed and when is versioned like software. Because it is software.

How is it measured?

Amplitude has measured the site since day one: page views, sessions, and outbound clicks. Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools watch indexing, and Bing matters more than it used to because its index feeds AI answers. Semrush tracks a short list of keywords, my name and the ideas I write about. Amplitude also tracks AI visibility: how the name and the ideas surface in ChatGPT and Google AI Overview answers, against a baseline recorded when the tracking started. A personal site gets the same measurement discipline as a business property. Every event also carries a bot classification, an is_bot flag and a user_agent_type category parsed from the user agent at load, so the reporting can filter to humans and break the automated traffic down by kind.

Amplitude also records sessions as replays, at full capture, so a visit that goes wrong can be watched instead of reconstructed from its events. Console output and failed requests are captured with the session, because the debugging record should show what the visitor’s browser saw, errors included. Text typed into any field stays masked, sessions the page classifies as bots are not recorded, and nothing in a replay carries a name or an email.

Who helps run it?

I work with a small bench of AI systems, each with a lane. Claude pressure-tests positioning. Grok checks claims against what is live right now. Gemini works through long documents and diligence. ChatGPT argues the other side. An AI agent coordinates the day-to-day: research, drafts, publishing, and regular checks on how the site and channels are doing.

Every draft comes back to me. I edit, I decide, I approve. Nothing publishes on a model’s say-so.

How does the site adapt?

The home page asks what brought you here and routes you to the part of the record that answers it. The leadership scorecard runs as a self-diagnostic in your browser; nothing you enter is stored. Underneath sits the structured record the sections below describe: one registry behind the answers, the search, and the assemblies. The record does not change. How you move through it does.

How does Ask my record work?

Every page on this site has a question box. It answers from one source: a public evidence registry, record.json, that holds every claim on this site as data, word for word, with its source page. The registry is the site, in machine-readable form.

A small AI model (Claude Haiku) reads the question and picks which records answer it. The model never writes the answer. Code assembles the reply from the registry verbatim and links the source. Numbers cannot drift, because the model never touches them. If no record answers, the box says so and points to the case studies. If a question is out of bounds (my employer beyond my published role, compensation, my family), it gets one fixed refusal, every time. Some exclusions are enforced in code, not just in instructions to the model.

There is one input, at the top of every page, and it is also the search box (next section). Ask a question and the answer renders right there, in place. The box knows which page you are on. It suggests questions that page can actually answer, prefers that page’s records when several could answer, and ends with the next step that fits the page. The nav’s Ask link just focuses the input.

Questions are kept for 90 days so I can read what people actually ask and improve the record. Email addresses and phone numbers are stripped automatically before anything is stored. The question log keeps no visitor identifiers. After 90 days the log deletes itself.

Cost is capped twice: a monthly ceiling in the code, and a spend limit where the model bill actually lands. There is a kill switch on the site side and on the server side.

Before any of this shipped, it had to pass a 20-case test set: ordinary questions, scope traps, and adversarial ones. The first run passed 17. The three failures were fixed in the open: a missing record, a selection preference, and an exclusion that needed code instead of instructions. The set, 22 cases today, has to pass in full before changes go live.

The walk-away pair, side by side. A walk-away test asks whether the bar declines what it cannot verify. The pair runs it both ways: the same subject asked twice, once where the fact is not published and once where it is.

The pair, verbatim

Both answers are verbatim from the live bar, and the pair now runs in the test set, which stands at 22 cases.

Question: “How many people were on the Sotheby’s team?”
Answer: “I don’t have published evidence for that. The full record is on this site, and the case studies are the fastest way through it.”

No Sotheby’s team size is published, so the bar names what it cannot verify and stops. It does not borrow the Lindblad team of 10. That fact is published, but it belongs to a different question.

Question: “What organic search growth did the Sotheby’s work produce?”
Answer: “Organic traffic at Sotheby’s rose 167% during Douglas Cirillo’s tenure leading the digital product and growth stack.” (Sotheby’s case study)

That fact is published, so the bar answers with the claim exactly as written and links its source. Nothing was added to the record for this test. Only the questions changed.

Build your own. Publish your record as data, with sources. Keep every claim verbatim. Let the model select, never write. Write your refusals in advance and make them fixed. Log questions with a short fuse. Cap the spend where the bill lands. Gate changes on a test set that includes people trying to break it.

How does search work?

The same input searches the site. It runs on Algolia, the on-site search and recommendations tool from my Sotheby’s stack, over a public index built from the published pages: titles, fixed summaries, headings. Nothing else. Restricted records never enter it, and results show the fixed summary rather than a raw snippet, so a figure cannot be separated from its scope. The index rebuilds on every publish, into a temporary index that swaps in only on success, so the site and the index never disagree.

Type a few words, get pages. Ask a full question, and the input routes to Ask My Record instead. Results lean toward the kind of page you are already on.

The writing list on the home page and the related essays under each article are served from the same index. Publish a new essay and the index rebuilds itself, so the lists update on their own. Lists show the three most relevant items, with a link through to the full list.

The one-line introductions above the writing lists are AI-generated. Each is written ahead of time from the listed essays’ own summaries, checked against them before use, and labeled. If a check fails, the list appears without one.

Search queries are measured by length and result count, never stored as text. Result clicks are sent to Algolia under the same anonymous ID that Amplitude uses, so I can read search behavior and site behavior together. No names, no emails, nothing that identifies a person.

How do the key points work?

Most articles and case studies on this site open with a Key points box. It is the second generated surface here, after the one-line introductions above the writing lists, and the first one built to be quoted: each set is written ahead of time from the page’s own text and nothing else, so an answer engine citing a line is citing the page itself.

Two checks run before any set is used. A mechanical check rejects a set when a number, a name, or a capitalized phrase in it does not appear in the page. A second model then reads for the drift a mechanical check cannot see: a figure detached from its scope, a role upgraded beyond what the page says, a cause the page never claimed, the author’s name repeated until a summary reads like a bio. A set that fails either check is discarded, and a page with no passing set carries no box. All twenty articles and case studies carry one today. The boxes are labeled AI-generated, and they are part of the indexable page on purpose.

How does the writing work?

Essays publish first on Substack, where the discussion lives. Each one also gets a redrafted edition here under Writing: a new framing against the record, question-shaped headings, and links into the case studies and practice pages. Never a verbatim copy. The site edition is the version built to be found. Substack keeps the conversation.

How do the record pages work?

Each record page is a frozen composition: blocks of approved text, assembled once and published as a static page. The proof blocks trace to the case studies, and the figures carry their scope with them. Nothing on these pages is generated at visit time. The writing lists update as new essays are approved. The proof does not change without re-approval.

How do compositions work?

Pick a problem on the home page and the site assembles the full picture for it on the spot: the intent frame, the proof, and the practice behind it, drawn from the same approved blocks as the record pages. Every assembly is checked by a validator before it can serve. A link can carry a composition, the address ends in a short code, but it cannot invent one: an unrecognized code serves the ordinary page. Nothing is written at visit time, and switching compositions off changes nothing underneath.

When no record answers a question directly, the same words are read as the problem they describe: a classifier matches them to the closest of the seven intents or, when no single intent fits, assembles a new combination from the same block library. A new combination passes the same validator before it serves, carries no share code, and lives only for that visit. The view is labeled as assembled for the problem stated. A description outside the site’s perimeter receives the same plain no-match as any other, so the boundary cannot be mapped by asking, and a description with no assembly to offer leaves the standard page in place.

Starting where you land

The home page is no longer the only place a view gets assembled. Land on a case study and the page builds a starting view around itself, out of what the page is not: one line that places the page in the arc of the record, the sibling case study that shares its thread, the principle the work runs on, the practice behind it, and the way to continue the conversation. The page’s own proof is deliberately absent. The page is its own proof, and it stands complete underneath; ‘View this page as published’ puts the published page back exactly as it was. Nothing is written for the occasion: the assembly is five published blocks, chosen in advance, in a fixed order.

Each page may draw only its own slice of the library: its anchor, its paired sibling’s proof, one principle, the practice blocks, and the contact block. Essays stay out, and so does every other case study. The principle blocks live outside the home library entirely: a view assembled on the home page can never select one, so a principle never renders twice, and the site’s own publishing rule stays here in the colophon, where it belongs. An assembly never links to the page it renders on: the validators reject a starting view that carries such a block, and the renderer strips the link if one ever slips through, leaving the text standing. The rules travel too: the same validators that check a free-form view check these, including the anchor rule, now generalized so a page’s own anchor block counts as the anchor when it leads.

An assembled view can be shared. Its address carries the block list itself, and the page re-checks that list against its slice before rendering anything, so a tampered or stale address yields a valid rearrangement of published blocks or the plain page. A default view writes no code to the address; only an assembly that differs from the starting view earns one. Fragments that are not codes, the section links a page already carries, are left alone.

Off switch: one flag per page type (COMPOSE_PAGES) returns every page of that type to exactly its published form.

The page rebuilds itself

The prompt field. A starting view is an invitation, not a destination: on the Lindblad Expeditions case study, a field under the assembly lets a visitor describe the view they need, and the page recomposes around it from the same published blocks, under the same validators. Every turn is validated whole before it renders. The history is a row of chips holding validated views, never the words that produced them, and the first chip is always the page as published. A prompt about another case study earns a pointer to that page. A prompt beyond any one page earns an offer, and the view widens only when the visitor confirms it. Ten prompts per session. The server keeps no session: the context travels with each request as validated specs, and the question log records outcomes, never a session.

Starting where you land, with what you have already read

When you come back to this site, its pages can rebuild themselves around what you have already opened here.

That memory is a small ledger kept in your own browser. It holds the names of pages and records you have opened, records Ask returned for you, the kinds of answers Ask gave, the intents you have picked, and the views you have built or looked at, each with a count and a date. It never holds the words you typed. Questions and prompts are answered or built from and then gone, exactly as they were before this feature existed.

There is no server-side activation copy. Amplitude, the measurement tool this site has used since launch, keeps its own record of activity on this site, for statistics, and nothing in that record is read back to shape a page. The ledger in your browser is the only memory a page here is ever rebuilt from.

Entries stop counting after 90 days at most and are deleted the next time you visit; depending on your browser, they may be kept for less time than that. You can read the ledger, remove a category from it, or clear it entirely, from any view it shaped and from this page. Clearing it does not change the anonymous device ID this browser uses for measurement, and it does not delete the measurement events already recorded for statistics.

If you are visiting from the European Union or the United Kingdom, this site asks before it keeps that ledger at all. Until you say yes, nothing goes into the ledger, and the pages build the way they do for a first visit. Whatever you choose, this browser keeps only your answer and when you gave it.

One address, every page

The machinery used to sit on top of each page: a search bar in the article, a prompt field inside one case study, receipts narrating from inside the view. It has moved into the frame. One composer now sits under the topline on every page, and it does every job: find a page, ask the record a question, and, where a page can rebuild itself, rebuild it. What a page builds still appears in the page. What accounts for it, the receipt, the list of views you can return to, the control over what this site remembers, now lives in the frame too, the same on every page, because it was never part of the content. The Lindblad prompt field is retired as a separate element; the composer does its job, and its sessions before today are the pilot’s final count. The pattern is the one chat interfaces made familiar: a fixed place to address the site, and a page that answers.

The page answers

The composer used to sort every line typed into it. A question went one way, a search another, and a request to rebuild the page had to fail both before it was heard. That sorting is gone. Type what you need, in the words you would use in a chat, and the site gives one response: an answer from the published record when you asked something, the page rebuilt around it when there is more to show, a pointer when another page covers it, and a plain no when the record does not hold it. Your prompts stay listed in the frame as you go, so the visit reads as one conversation. The facts did not loosen. Every claim still comes from the record, word for word, and the same gates still decide what the site may say.

How is the site put together?

Three layers, one rule: a model can select and arrange. It cannot invent.

The record layer is static: plain HTML pages and one data file, record.json, holding every approved claim with its source. GitHub Pages serves them.

The retrieval layer answers. Keyword searches go to an Algolia index built only from the published pages. Questions go to a small function on AWS: a Claude model picks the records that answer, and code assembles the reply from those records word for word. Nothing in the answer is written by the model.

The generative layer is narrow on purpose. The introductions over the writing lists are written ahead of time by an offline script, from the listed essays’ own summaries. A mechanical checker and a second model review each line before it is stored, and the site serves the stored file. Nothing is generated while a visitor waits.

Measurement runs through Amplitude across the whole site. No names, no emails. Each layer can be switched off on its own; the static record serves either way.

What are the rules?

Settled facts only. Every number on this site traces to work I actually did. One change at a time, then measure. And the harness checks from my writing, Brand Out-of-Bounds, Contextual Switch, and Guardrails, apply to the team that builds this site too. Gates, not prompts.

The argument of my writing is that a small team with a real foundation outruns a big team on a bloated stack. This site is that argument, running in production.

Where is this going?

The record does not change. What changes is how much of the experience around it can assemble itself, under the same gates. The set lives in the record.

Next

The question log gets its first review: a month of real visitor questions, audited in December. What the bar refused, and what it could not answer, becomes new published evidence or new blocks for the library, so the record grows where visitors actually press on it.

Then: the manifest

When the same novel assembly keeps recurring in the log, it gets a spot check, and the ones that hold up join the manifest as permanent compositions, with share codes of their own. Until then, a new combination lives only for the visit that asked for it.
