Self-Diagnostic

The Digital Experience Leadership Scorecard

The Digital Experience Leadership Scorecard is Douglas Cirillo’s self-diagnostic for whoever owns a brand’s digital experience: the leader running it, or the executive deciding whether it is being run well. Twenty statements, five dimensions, scored on what you can prove. It runs in your browser. Nothing you enter is stored or sent anywhere.

How to score it

Score what you can prove, not what you believe. A statement earns a 2 only if you could show the evidence to a skeptic in the room. If you have to explain why it is basically true, it is a 1. If it is not true yet, it is a 0, and a 0 is useful. It tells you where the sequence starts.

The scorecard follows the order I work in and have written about as Builder OS: people and data first, AI second, score kept in business currency. Each statement is 0 (not true), 1 (partly true), or 2 (true and provable). Each dimension totals 8. The full scorecard totals 40.

1. Data foundation

Customer identity is resolved across our systems. One person is one profile, wherever they show up.

One governed schema defines our events, and every team instruments to it.

Who a customer is stays separate from what they did this morning. Stable facts and fresh behavior are not stored as the same thing.

Our customer data platform has a named owner and a roadmap. It runs as a product, not a project with an end date.

What good looks like: any team can answer “what do we know about this customer” from one place, and trust the answer.

Red flag: two teams pull the same metric, get different numbers, and the meeting turns into an argument about the data instead of the decision.

Dimension score: 0 / 8

2. Experimentation and measurement

Every test names its objective and one primary metric before it runs.

Results are reported in business currency: bookings, revenue, share, sales. Not clicks, not impressions.

Our core metrics are defined once, in writing, and mean the same thing in every dashboard.

When the stack, the model, or the vendor changes, we re-test. We do not assume results earned under the old setup still hold.

What good looks like: a test readout settles an argument in the room, because the definitions were agreed before the test ran.

Red flag: the testing backlog is a list of page tweaks that nobody can connect to a number the business cares about.

Dimension score: 0 / 8

3. AI readiness and answer discoverability

The data underneath our AI is governed before the AI layer goes on top. It is not cleaned up afterward.

Every automated or AI-driven system passes three gates before it reaches production: Brand Out-of-Bounds, Contextual Switch, and Guardrails.

Our stable facts, what we are and what we offer, are structured and machine-readable, kept separate from details that change daily.

We have checked what the major answer engines say about us, by name and by category, and we know whether they get it right.

What good looks like: an agent asking about your category finds consistent, current facts and represents the brand accurately.

Red flag: the AI pilot demos beautifully on clean sample data while the production data underneath it is fragmented.

Dimension score: 0 / 8

4. Team and operating model

The people who will run the system helped design it: the schema, the definitions, the roadmap.

Every platform we depend on has a named owner inside the company. Nothing important is “the vendor’s job.”

Teams join the program because they saw proof from a team already in it, not because they were told to comply.

Schema and definition discipline holds even when a campaign wants an exception.

What good looks like: if the leader walked away, the function would keep running, because the team built it and believes it is theirs.

Red flag: adoption is handled as a communications problem, with a launch plan and a training deck, after the build is already done.

Dimension score: 0 / 8

5. Experience coherence

A customer moving between our site, app, email, and ads meets one brand that remembers them the same way in each place.

Martech and data strategy sit underneath the experience and serve it. They are not the goal.

Personalization and decisioning are aimed at named objectives. Nothing runs just because the tool can do it.

Search and recommendations on our own properties run on the same governed data as everything else, not a separate feed with separate rules.

What good looks like: the experience holds together across channels because the foundation underneath it is shared.

Red flag: each channel team runs its own stack, its own data, and its own version of the customer.

Dimension score: 0 / 8

Reading your score

Your score: 0 / 40. Band: .

A low score is not a verdict on the leader. Most digital functions were assembled under pressure, one tool and one campaign at a time, and the seams show. What matters is the order you fix them in. Build with the people who will run it. Data first, AI second. Gates, not prompts. Outcomes, not screens. Take the scorecard again in two quarters and score it the same hard way. The number moving is the proof.
