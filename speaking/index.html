Speaking and Media

Speaking and media

Douglas Cirillo is Head of Digital Experience and Growth at Vanguard and a digital product leader whose work spans travel, media, live entertainment, and consumer services. His work focuses on building digital and data functions, establishing customer data foundations, and connecting experience decisions to measurable outcomes. He writes Observations from the Middle Seat and is available for selected speaking and media engagements. He is based in West Chester, Pennsylvania, outside Philadelphia.

The talk

In September 2019, Douglas Cirillo and Richard McGovern(opens in new tab) presented “How Sotheby’s Transformed a 275-Year Old Company into a Data Innovator” at Synapse, Segment’s conference. The session was also presented at Gartner.

Session abstract (2019): “Learn how Sotheby’s, the global leader in art and luxury commerce, replaced a legacy data ecosystem with one that supports data-driven decision making and scalability. In this session, Douglas Cirillo and Richard McGovern will discuss how the 275-year-old auction house approached digital transformation and established a unified data infrastructure that provides greater integration between platforms and systems, more actionable insights, and deeper consumer personalization.”

What the talk covers: why a 275-year-old auction house could not personalize or measure anything while its customer data sat in fragments, and what replaced it. One governed data foundation, a customer data platform connected across 23 systems with a single warehouse underneath, and an adoption model built on earning it: start with one team, ship visible wins, and let the rest ask to join.

Transcript

This is the transcript of the talk Douglas Cirillo and Richard McGovern gave at Synapse 2019, Segment's user conference, held in San Francisco in September 2019. It covers how Sotheby's built a single, unified view of its customer data, and the adoption work that made it stick. Figures and examples are as of 2019 and are transcribed as spoken. Introduction: a 275-year-old auction house goes digital (00:00)

Douglas Cirillo: Hey, how's everybody doing? Good. Thank you for joining. Before we dive into what we've been doing from a digital transformation side, we have a video that we put together to kind of give everybody here a little context into Sotheby's and what we've been up to for the last 275 years. I know that not everybody's super familiar with us, or if you are, you're maybe more familiar with the real estate play, but I'm going to play this and then we'll jump in.

[Video plays] A Sotheby's brand video: specialists and artists talk about discovering and selling great works of art, cut with live auction footage and hammer prices.

Douglas Cirillo: So we have established ourselves over the last 275 years as the global leader in art, and in a more modern sense, powered by not only our expertise but also by technology and data. One of the challenges that we have, especially in the commerce space, is that we're not really what you would consider a traditional SKU or inventoried business. So the big challenge is, how do you scale bespoke? As we lean more into digital and technology to really scale our business on both sides. And when I say both sides, we obviously have the selling component to our company, where people bid and interact to buy luxury art and other high-value items, but we also have to consign, and we have inventory-getting opportunities that we need to achieve at scale.

Unlike most traditional businesses, we traffic highly unique items: people that have an understanding of it, with people that are interested in buying it. So how do you connect those two worlds together at scale? Traditionally, what we've done is we have a whole department and whole sets of teams that really have one-on-one relationships with some of our higher-value customers. But as we continue to grow our business globally, and we have not only the three locations that we build upon and have headquarters in New York, Hong Kong, and London today, but we also have global business and global offices worldwide, how do we perform outreach for business-getting opportunities on the consignment side, and how do we find new customers and new people interested in unique objects?

The goal: a single, unified view of the customer (04:05)

Douglas Cirillo: So what we needed to do was step back and really wrap our heads around establishing a single unified view and source of truth for all consumer data. Over the last couple of years, Sotheby's has invested a lot into a complete replatforming across the board, but where we focused to start was on the martech consumer interaction points through digital channels. And what we wanted to do was come around: one, aligning our teams around a common view and understanding of what the customer is; two, consolidate the technology stacks and systems by which we're leveraging to make business decisions, and have that kind of understanding enable and consolidate what those systems are, and coordinate between all of those teams in a way that we haven't been able to in the past. Because, as we'll get to in a minute, it's just been fairly difficult, because we never thought about things in a data-technology-first perspective, and inform our users internally so that they have a good idea what's happening in traditional senses offline, and marry that with the online, and really empower our internal business stakeholders, as well as the consumers, to really self-manage their relationship with Sotheby's and make us more accessible than I think we probably have been perceived in the past.

This picture represents where we were about a year and a half ago, and this is just a representation of our martech ecosystem. Again, we're still not convinced that this is completely comprehensive, and we've anonymized this a bit, but you can see that this is a really jumbled, difficult-to-manage architecture. And it's a mix of some of our front-end applications, our mobile app, our web applications, our marketing systems, our proprietary CRM that we've established over time, etc.

So what this really presents in terms of a challenge is: one, you have multiple decoupled platforms. All of these platforms have been suitable over time to satisfy different business needs in a traditional sense, but the sharing of data didn't really happen, or was only happening on an ad hoc and semi-coupled or semi-integrated basis. A lot of architectural challenges, because there was no ownership, no oversight over this, no ownership over the central understanding or identity around users, or what it is that we were tracking, from system to system, team to team, and what was important globally to the company. And all of that leads to non-scalable legacy infrastructure. So as we double down in terms of leveraging digital channels to really understand and drive business in the future, we needed to really step back and think about, how can we do that? How can we set up an ecosystem and architecture that really enables a digital approach and understanding of our consumers?

So we got together about a year ago, in the fall, in September 2018, all the major business stakeholders and technical stakeholders and leaders that really touched any consumer data, whether it's an application on the front end or a system in the background, to not only align around the vision statement of establishing a single source, but also to articulate, in a kind of blue-sky way, where we wanted to go. And this is where we ended up, from a proposal standpoint. So what you'll see here is that, one, we wanted to go towards a much more modular view of the world. What you'll see there is that we've kind of bucketed our digital engagement applications, whether it's web or native application, what we're doing on the sales or remarketing side, our transactional platforms, and again, when we say transactional we're talking about bidding and consignment, and then our reporting tier, the analytics platforms that we have, and we have multiple to serve different business needs, and establish a warehouse, which we really didn't have in a modern cloud infrastructure sense. And we were positioning Segment to be kind of the control point and the governance layer when it came to integrations, federation, and management of all of that.

The cost of the old way: tag specs and duplicated work (07:52)

Douglas Cirillo: What we were hoping to achieve out of this, and what we ultimately did achieve out of this, was projected cost savings. Since we had multiple teams in the past managing all these integrations, managing all these tag specs, making sure they were aligned, or supporting different integrations, we were able to project and recognize savings from a manpower perspective of 200 hours of implementation time and approximately 60 hours a month of ongoing support. Now, when you free up the engineering teams to not have to put that much time aside to manage integrations, we were able to recognize an uptick in velocity. So now those teams could feature on feature development around core business objectives and needs, and not have to manage all of these ad hoc data plays that we had in house, in a legacy viewpoint. And then we're able to execute quicker than we have before, and be able to pivot, because now everybody has a centralized view of what consumers are doing.

What this translates to, in a Segment point of view: Segment thinks of things in kind of the notion of sources and destinations. So this is sort of the graph that you saw before, visualized from a Segment POV. On the left-hand side you'll see a list of sources, and that's a combination of web, native applications, marketing platforms, all the platforms that you saw in those buckets. In the middle you'll see the schema management, or the schemas that articulated and developed for each of those, and then how those map to different destinations. And then you'll see some of the destinations are themselves a source back. So now, wherever actions are performed downstream, whether in their omnichannel marketing platforms or CRM systems, any interactions there can be federated right back into the ecosystem and help inform other plays. So, fairly powerful. One of the things that this allows for is not only a way to organize all of our information, but also to provide a layer of governance that we haven't had before.

Richard McGovern: Yeah, thanks, Doug. So before Segment, maintaining tracking consistency across multiple web experiences and applications was a significant challenge. Multiple analytics platforms, marketing tools, and pseudo data warehouses all relied on disparate and often inconsistent schemas. In some cases, it's really doubled or tripled the amount of work needed to define, reconcile, and ultimately implement tracking. Working with Segment as our CDP has allowed us to dramatically streamline this effort, since we can now rely on a single tracking plan for multiple downstream destinations. This also really pushed us through the effort of defining our tracking plan in a way that aligned against our strategic KPIs. We use tools like Segment's Protocols to manage our tracking plan, which increases drastically the level of data governance that we're able to apply. In particular, we can help automate the quality assurance and data governance process by flagging any properties or events that aren't already predefined as part of our schema, and actually filtering them out, so we're not sending bad or erroneous data downstream to our destinations. And we can even do this on a per-destination basis, which is extremely helpful.

But all of this consistency and unification not only saves us time and resources, it makes data democratization across the business much more feasible. When everybody is relying on the same data, it means reporting and analytics is more reliable and feasible. And it really shifts the conversation from the anecdotal to the insightful. And importantly, it makes adoption across the enterprise much easier.

Adoption is the key element (11:48)

Douglas Cirillo: So adoption is a key element to this. We could talk about our blue sky, where we want to go, but especially after the conference, going back and working with non-technical stakeholders, they don't necessarily care about the hows and whys. They have business needs that they need satisfied and not disrupted currently. So what we decided to do, to move down the path to modernizing our data infrastructure, was, one, to start small. As part of our replatforming, one of the things that we were doing was re-engineering our front-end web experience, and on the web already there was a good, healthy amount of tracking and tagging that was already in place, to serve various marketing, reporting, editorial reporting, advertising needs and teams. So what we did was, we took, and at the time this was, and still is, our reporting source of truth, our Adobe integration, our Adobe Analytics tag spec, which is probably the most comprehensive that we had on our front end, and recreated that for the new front end that we were developing.

One, we didn't want to disrupt. We wanted to tag-team it with our business stakeholders on the marketing side, who leveraged the Adobe reporting for pushing out performance information to the rest of the company. So we didn't want to disrupt what it is that they were doing. But we also wanted to empower not only their team, but any other team that was leveraging web front-end behavior, in a consolidated way. So we recreated the Adobe tagging architecture, leveraging Segment's analytics.js, integrating that web front end, cut over without disrupting any existing reporting needs. And then what happened, because we saw for that one use case, all the other teams now, that had subsets of tagging or subsets of data that Adobe was collecting, were now free to integrate with a greater scope and wealth of information than they had before, aligned with the marketing team, who was already collecting the information via the Adobe spec.

So as we worked to add the advertising tech to it, other editorial reporting tech, and again, some teams were using Google Analytics, some others, but now that all those teams had access to the same information, that was normalized for the first time, they were able to really work and coordinate together. And it shifted, to Rich's point, kind of the conversation around whose is the source of truth in reporting, to now they were aligned. They had the same view of the world, and they were working together and collaborating in a way that they hadn't had a chance to before. And what's great is that, because they were able to do that, they started evangelizing this approach for us. And they started to go out to other systems and stakeholders, demanding, not really demanding, maybe that's a little too strong, but asking if they can integrate, let's say, the transactional platform for understanding, authentication platforms for understanding, so that they have more sources of information. And what's great, as they evangelized and as these other teams began to integrate with the Segment ecosystem, it kind of had that snowball effect, where you prove the value to those teams, and then more and more kind of jump on over time.

And where this has led us to: we had a conference in September, and at the end of that we didn't really know, and we wanted to have a replatforming done within a year. We were able to complete it within months. So it was pretty impactful. And it just goes to show you, really, the way to get around adoption is to start small, show don't tell, and demonstrate value, because when you demonstrate value, then you're really helping to enable those other teams. And all of this adoption and collaboration has really been enabled by the unified set of tracking that we've been able to achieve.

Richard McGovern: And all of this helps to make product development and marketing technology deployment much more modular. So this, of course, enables greater speed to market for various destination integrations, and it's a lot more scalable. All of this goes hand in hand with operational efficiency. So less manual time is needed to cleanse and reconcile data on a per-system basis, and less time and fewer resources are required to do the implementation. So the old days, where a full development cycle was required for us to enable tracking for a single analytics platform, are fortunately long gone.

Rethinking the data architecture (Richard McGovern) (16:09)

Richard McGovern: As Doug has kind of indicated, this has allowed us to really rethink and re-envision our data architecture holistically. And as we last year met as stakeholders and leaders from across the organization, we really aligned around this future-state objective to create a data warehouse that would serve as a single source of truth for all of our consumer data. We were able to achieve this warehouse, based on modern cloud infrastructure, in literally just a couple days, leveraging our Segment data, and very shortly thereafter we were actually able to start rolling out new reports to the business.

So I can show you one example, with just some placeholder data for exemplification purposes. This is a report that we rolled out, focused on our online auctions, from in particular a media and marketing perspective, based on all the Segment data that we'd stood up. And it shows, for example, top-level traffic to each sale. It shows where users are coming from in terms of traffic sources, geographic distribution, a notion of a conversion funnel, so how people are moving through the product experience, through the bidding flow, and then, of course, bids per day by device and location. And so previously, all of this data existed, but it resided in multiple systems, and it was really only accessible to a few fairly technical users. Now literally anyone in the business can access this information.

Since then, we've of course continued to expand these reporting use cases, leveraging the plethora of data that we've been able to collect through Segment. So we're looking at things like how various features and products on the site are converting users, what the drop-off is like. To give you an example, we're looking at conversion and drop-off over time. But more deeply than that, we've been able to understand, in a much better way, how users are engaging with our products cross-device. So how are users transacting on, and engaging with content on, native mobile applications and the responsive web? This opens the door for a lot in the future, in particular around personalization.

Growth: registration, email, and content (19:11)

Douglas Cirillo: Yeah. So there's a lot that we've been able to achieve, just by closing the existing gaps that we have and aligning businesses around a common set of data, supporting existing business needs. So here you can see, over the course of the last year and a half, two years, with no real net change in business operations, what we've been able to do. Because we're able to support decision-making better, because we have a central view of our digital customers at least, we've been able to better target on social and grow our social audience. Our registration, and understanding our registration conversion, that's grown exponentially. Our email, we've been able to, and I'll go into a little bit in the next slide, but our email campaigns and the way that we market to people and known users has really driven, and become a revenue driver for us. Our editorial content, because we can plan better, not only proactively, based around what consumers are looking for in our space, but also react quicker and know what content works, we've been able to more efficiently create draft content. So the growth around our editorial consumption has been really, really huge. And we do pride ourselves on a content-and-commerce strategy, in terms of leveraging all forms of media, specifically video, as kind of our top of funnel, to convert new users and to re-engage existing users. Our videos in particular have been a huge growth channel for us. We've grown exponentially over the last two years, and we were already at 100% against Q2, this past year versus last. So we're blowing everything away, and doubling almost year-over-year in terms of video consumption, both on and off platform. And then we've had kind of record traffic, as a downstream effect of all of this. Huge numbers. And again, nothing completely different than what we've been doing. It's been doing smarter, and enabling our business stakeholders to make decisions, and better decisions, faster.

So this is the email use case. Our email and marketing teams leverage a platform that has a fair bit of ML behind it, and has the ability to personalize, but again, you can only really personalize and get into that once you have good data to support that. So as we synced up the Segment data to our remarketing platform downstream, they were able to really start building these dynamic campaigns, and personalize them, not only the distribution and the sending, but also the content. So to distill that, they essentially send half the emails that they did before, and have doubled or tripled their open rates and click-through rates, which is huge. So it kind of takes the decision-making, on what it is that we're doing when it comes to consumer engagement, out of the anecdotal internal business leaders, and really puts it, empowers the consumers, to really drive what we should be doing, and when we should be surfacing up recommendations and communication.

What we'd like to do is to extend the learnings that we have from our email campaigns to our web front end. That includes and encapsulates both what we're doing from recommending sales and lots that are coming up, to new and known users, as well as even personalizing our search experience. So instead of having these internal rules, for either ranking search or what it is that we're promoting, let that be consumer-driven. So, no one rule to rule them all anymore. It's really down to a per-person perspective, based on how we're weighting things from a business value versus what the consumers are actually interested in driving.

More mature testing (22:01)

Douglas Cirillo: And it also allows us to start really doing more mature testing. Since we are federating, and have that common set of data across all these platforms, including our testing platform, now we don't have that argument over what the KPIs are, what the shifts are. Everybody is speaking the same language, and understands, and can establish what a common set of KPIs are, based on a common understanding and dictionary of properties and events that we're tracking across all systems.

So, improving the client relationship through insight. One of the platforms that we had bucketed was our CRM. So we have an old proprietary CRM that we've been leaning on for about 30, 35 years. And what this has really opened up the door for us is to really migrate to a modern cloud-based CRM platform. So not only leveraging existing and legacy understanding around our clients, existing clients, what their sales history is, what their consignment history is, but also, as our digital engagement grows and becomes the largest channel by which users interact with Sotheby's, we can inform our sales and specialists, in real time, what consumers are doing, what their interests are, whether explicit or implicit, based on what they're viewing online, in sales or content, by what they're interested in consigning. So you kind of marry the legacy understanding that our inside specialists have, and the expertise they have, with what consumers are actually doing, and what users are doing from a lead perspective. And it really helps them, from an outreach perspective, and to drive business, and to drive both sides of the transactional coin.

What's next: closing the gaps beyond martech (24:03)

Douglas Cirillo: So what we went over, when we started a year ago, was looking particularly and specifically at the martech side of the world, how people are engaging with us on digital channels, since we needed to set a foundation there, so that we can, over the next five years, grow those different channels aggressively. But what we want to start looking at now, that we've solved for the martech side of the coin in a relatively short amount of time than we would have traditionally been able to do, is how do we close the gaps that exist outside the martech space. So over the next couple of years, we want to really scale out, or look to scale out, what we've done here, to close the gaps that we have on syncing and interlocking our cataloging systems, and bringing and introducing that into this ecosystem, our research platforms, and our financial. So financial being very key, because that has all the records around both digital transactions, as well as offline traditional transactions. So once we have all of that information synced up, you can start really doing powerful stuff, especially down in a warehouse, since Segment, via Personas, acts as an ETL around that. So in terms of being able to look up, and help BI do their job better, whether it's analyzing what's happening today or looking at things historically, and having that perspective, there's a lot of power that can be done there. So we're entering the discovery period there. But what's great is, because that essentially unifies all of our enterprise systems into one common data architecture, one set of properties, master property list. Really exciting stuff. There's a lot of work ahead. So I think we've only really scratched the surface, in terms of the potential and what we can do here, but there's a lot that we're going to be going for in the next year and a half.

So, for a takeaway, I would say, especially for any business that's starting down this path, and we're a particularly challenging use case, just given the uniqueness of our business, but I think this can apply to anybody that's going through, or starting down, the road of transformation. One is, start small. Find those high-value, low-effort, low-hanging-fruit technical cases. In our case, we used the web tagging integration as kind of our V1. Show don't tell. Demonstrate the value back to the business stakeholders and teams you're helping to enable. And again, once you demonstrate the value back, they become the evangelizers for you, and are able to then collaborate, because you've democratized the data, and they can work together in ways that they haven't before. So this is the approach that we took. We've seen a lot of success. We got a lot done in a year, which is incredible, but there's a lot of work ahead. But really appreciate your time.
